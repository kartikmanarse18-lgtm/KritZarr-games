FREDDY JACKSON
HMS Daedalus, 1919 — A Paranormal Investigation

The Ghost of Freddy Jackson

In the spring of 1919, a squadron of Royal Air Force airmen gathered at the HMS Daedalus training facility for a routine group photograph. They arranged themselves in neat rows, caps pressed, uniforms immaculate, the war behind them and peace ahead. But when the photograph was developed, something impossible emerged from the chemical bath—a face that had no business being there. Behind the fourth man from the left in the back row, peering between two airmen, was the unmistakable visage of Freddy Jackson, an air mechanic who had been killed by an airplane propeller two days earlier. His funeral had taken place on the very day the photograph was taken. He had come anyway.

The photograph lay dormant for over half a century, tucked away in the archives of a man who would become one of Britain's most decorated military officers and its most unlikely paranormal investigator. Sir Victor Goddard, Air Chief Marshal and Knight Commander of the Bath, had a secret life. Beyond the medals and the commands, he was a man who had seen things that defied explanation—ghostly airfields that appeared from nowhere, dreams that predicted crashes, and now, a photograph that seemed to capture the impossible: a dead man attending his own squadron's portrait session.

Freddy Jackson had been an ordinary air mechanic at the HMS Daedalus naval seaplane training school at Lee-on-Solent, Hampshire. On a routine day in April 1919, he walked heedlessly into the arc of a spinning propeller. The blade caught him with lethal force, ending his life instantly. His comrades buried him with full military honors. Two days later, on the day of his funeral, the squadron assembled for a group photograph to commemorate their service in the Great War. They stood in the hangar doorway, the English spring light falling across their faces, and smiled for the camera. None of them noticed the extra man in the back row.

When the photograph was developed, the squadron members gathered around to examine the proof. Their eyes moved across the familiar faces—friends, comrades, brothers in arms. Then someone pointed. There, behind the airman standing fourth from the left in the top row, was a face that made the blood run cold. It was Freddy Jackson. Bald, balding, unmistakable. He was not wearing a cap like the others. His face was slightly transparent, slightly blurred, as if caught between two worlds. The men who had known him best confirmed it without hesitation: that was Freddy. He had come to say goodbye, or perhaps he simply did not know he was dead.

The image would not be made public until 1975, when Sir Victor Goddard published it in his book "Flight Towards Reality"—a treatise on extrasensory perception, spiritualism, and the survival of consciousness after death. By then, most of the men in the photograph were dead themselves. But the face of Freddy Jackson remained, frozen in silver halide, an eternal witness to a mystery that has never been solved. Skeptics have offered explanations: double exposure, a man who stepped into frame at the wrong moment, pareidolia, photo manipulation. But none of these explanations fully account for what the men of Goddard's squadron saw that day, or what millions have seen since. The ghost of Freddy Jackson stands behind them still, the uninvited squad member who refused to leave.

He published the Freddy Jackson photograph in his 1975 book "Flight Towards Reality," a treatise on ESP, spiritualism, and consciousness after death. He had multiple paranormal experiences throughout his life.

Location

HMS Daedalus — The Training Facility

The Royal Naval Air Station at Lee-on-Solent, Hampshire, later known as HMS Daedalus. This was a seaplane training facility during and after World War I. It was here that Freddy Jackson worked as an air mechanic and where he was killed by a propeller. The squadron photograph was taken in one of the hangar doorways.

Primary Subject

Freddy Jackson — The Mechanic

An air mechanic serving at the HMS Daedalus training facility. He was accidentally killed by an airplane propeller in April 1919, two days before the squadron photograph was taken. His funeral took place on the same day the photograph was captured. Registry records confirm a George Frederick Jackson died in the RAF Aeroplane Repair Section.

Witnesses

The men who served alongside Freddy Jackson and who were present for the group photograph. Their testimony forms a crucial part of the paranormal claim, as they knew Jackson's features intimately.

Publication

Case Timeline

Every documented event from Freddy Jackson's death to the photograph's publication and the century of investigation that followed.

World War I Era
The Great War & HMS Daedalus

The Royal Naval Air Station at Lee-on-Solent, Hampshire, serves as a critical training facility for seaplane pilots and air mechanics during World War I. The station, later designated HMS Daedalus, becomes home to hundreds of RAF personnel who train in aircraft maintenance, repair, and flight operations over the English Channel.

April 1919
The Fatal Accident

Air Mechanic Freddy Jackson is killed in a tragic accident at the HMS Daedalus facility. He walks heedlessly into the arc of a spinning airplane propeller, suffering fatal injuries. The accident occurs just two days before the squadron's scheduled group photograph. His comrades are devastated by the sudden loss of a fellow mechanic.

April 1919 — Day of Funeral
The Funeral & The Photograph

On the very day of Freddy Jackson's funeral, his squadron assembles for a group photograph at the HMS Daedalus facility. The airmen arrange themselves in rows in a hangar doorway, wearing their military caps and uniforms. The photograph is taken with a large-format camera using a long exposure typical of the era. No one notices anything unusual during the session. The funeral and the photograph occur on the same day—a coincidence that would later fuel the paranormal narrative.

April 1919 — Post-Development
The Discovery

When the photographic plate is developed and printed, squadron members gather to examine the proof. As they scan the familiar faces, someone points to the back row, fourth from the left. Behind that airman, peering between two heads, is an extra face—a bald or balding man without a cap. The men who knew Freddy Jackson best immediately identify it as him. He is not wearing a cap like everyone else. His features are slightly transparent, almost ghostly. The squadron is shaken. Some are convinced their dead comrade has returned. Others search for rational explanations.

1919–1975
Fifty-Six Years in the Shadows

The photograph remains private for over half a century. It is kept in the personal collection of Sir Victor Goddard, who was present at HMS Daedalus during this period. Goddard rises through the RAF ranks, eventually becoming Air Chief Marshal and receiving numerous honors including Knight Commander of the Bath. Throughout his military career, he experiences multiple paranormal events—including a famous 1935 incident where he witnesses an abandoned Scottish airfield fully operational and painted yellow, years before it is actually restored. These experiences shape his later interest in spiritualism and consciousness.

1935
Goddard's Paranormal Vision

While serving as a Wing Commander, Victor Goddard flies to inspect the abandoned RAF Drem airfield in Scotland. He finds it derelict, with cattle grazing on grass growing through cracked tarmac. Later, caught in heavy rain, he returns to Drem for his bearings. As he approaches, the rain abruptly clears to bright sunlight—and the airfield appears fully repaired, operational, with aircraft painted bright yellow. Four years later, at the outbreak of World War II, RAF Drem is indeed restored and its planes are painted yellow. This vision cements Goddard's belief in extrasensory perception and premonition.

1975
Flight Towards Reality

Sir Victor Goddard publishes his book "Flight Towards Reality," a treatise on ESP, spiritual healing, and the survival of consciousness after death. For the first time, he makes the Freddy Jackson photograph public. The image immediately captures the imagination of paranormal investigators, spiritualists, and skeptics worldwide. It becomes one of the most famous ghost photographs ever taken, cited in countless books, documentaries, and investigations. The photograph's release sparks decades of analysis, debate, and competing theories.

1975–2000s
The Golden Age of Paranormal Interest

The Freddy Jackson photograph becomes a staple of paranormal literature and television. It appears in books about ghosts, hauntings, and the afterlife. Documentary filmmakers feature it in programs about unexplained phenomena. The image is reproduced, enhanced, zoomed, and analyzed by both believers and skeptics. The story of the uninvited squad member who didn't know he was dead becomes part of popular paranormal culture. Multiple versions of the photograph circulate, some enhanced, some retouched, some with varying levels of contrast and clarity.

2000s–2010s
Skeptical Re-examination

Modern skeptical investigators begin to scrutinize the Freddy Jackson photograph with new tools and perspectives. Researchers at Skeptic.com and Metabunk.org conduct detailed analyses. They discover discrepancies in the traditional narrative: the only confirmed RAF death record for a George Frederick Jackson shows he died in April 1918 at a hospital in Sheffield—miles from Lee-on-Solent and a year before the photograph was supposedly taken. The traditional story of Jackson dying two days before the photo is called into question. Skeptics propose alternative explanations: double exposure, a man stepping into frame during a long exposure, pareidolia, or even deliberate photo manipulation by Goddard himself.

2010s–Present
The Digital Age & Ongoing Mystery

The Freddy Jackson photograph enters the digital age, shared across social media, Reddit, TikTok, and YouTube. New generations discover the image and debate its authenticity. Some users claim to see a second ghostly face on the opposite side of the photograph. Others overlay the "ghost" face with the airman in front, noting striking similarities that suggest it might be the same person captured in two positions during a long exposure. The photograph is uploaded to Wikimedia Commons and other archives, making the original more accessible than ever. Despite over a century of analysis, no definitive explanation has been accepted by all parties. The ghost of Freddy Jackson continues to haunt the back row of that 1919 squadron portrait.

Present Day
The Mystery Endures

Multiple theories continue to compete for acceptance: genuine paranormal manifestation, double exposure during a long-exposure photograph, a squad member who stepped into frame at the wrong moment, pareidolia creating a face where none exists, or deliberate enhancement/manipulation by Sir Victor Goddard to support his spiritualist beliefs. The original photographic plate has never been located for modern forensic analysis. The men who could have confirmed or denied the story are all dead. Freddy Jackson—whether ghost, accident, or illusion—remains the most famous uninvited squad member in photographic history.

The Evidence

Physical findings, photographic analysis, and the inexplicable details that define the Freddy Jackson mystery.

Critical Evidence

The Ghost Photograph Itself

The 1919 squadron photograph shows approximately 20-25 airmen arranged in three rows. In the back row, behind the fourth airman from the left, an extra face is visible. The face is bald or balding, lacks a military cap (unlike all other squad members), and appears slightly transparent or faded compared to the other faces. The expression is calm, almost smiling. The face is positioned between two heads in the row behind, suggesting it belongs to someone standing in a position where no one should be.

Documentary Evidence

The Death Registry Record

Skeptical investigators discovered RAF death registry number 591269 for a George Frederick Jackson who worked in the RAF Aeroplane Repair Section. However, this record shows he died on April 13, 1918 at the 3rd Northern General Hospital in Sheffield—approximately 240 miles from Lee-on-Solent and a full year before the photograph was supposedly taken in 1919. This discrepancy challenges the traditional narrative of Jackson dying just two days before the photo. No confirmed record of a Freddy Jackson dying at HMS Daedalus in April 1919 has been located.

Forensic Analysis

The Face Overlay Comparison

Skeptical researchers have performed digital overlays comparing the "ghost" face with the airman standing in front of it (fourth from left, back row). The facial features show striking similarities—same nose shape, same eye spacing, same jawline. This has led to the theory that the "ghost" is actually the same airman, captured in two different positions during the long exposure typical of early 20th-century photography. If he removed his cap and shifted position during the exposure, he would appear twice in the final image.

Visual Evidence

The Second Face Phenomenon

Some observers claim to see a second ghostly face on the opposite side of the photograph, near the right edge of the back row. This has been attributed to pareidolia—the human tendency to perceive faces in random patterns. However, believers argue that if one face is a genuine ghost, the second might be as well. Skeptics counter that pareidolia would naturally produce multiple "faces" in a complex image of heads and uniforms. The second face is far less distinct than the primary Jackson apparition.
