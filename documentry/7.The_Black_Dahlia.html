BLACK DAHLIA
Los Angeles, 1947 — A True Crime Investigation

She Was Called The Black Dahlia

"At first, I thought it was a mannequin," Betty Bersinger said. She had been pushing her three-year-old daughter in a stroller down Norton Avenue when she saw something white in the grass. But mannequins don't bleed. Mannequins aren't cut in half. And mannequins don't have Glasgow smiles carved into their faces.

It began with a young woman chasing a dream. Elizabeth Short, a 22-year-old brunette with piercing blue eyes and a fondness for black dresses, arrived in Hollywood in 1946 with aspirations of stardom. She had come from Massachusetts, from a life of modest means, to a city that promised glitter and fame to anyone beautiful enough. She stayed in cheap hotels, worked as a waitress, and told stories—so many stories—about movie contracts that never existed and romances that never happened.

They called her the Black Dahlia, a nickname born from the 1946 film The Blue Dahlia and her penchant for dark clothing. But the name would soon take on a far darker meaning. On January 9, 1947, she was last seen at the Biltmore Hotel in downtown Los Angeles. She had been dropped off by Robert "Red" Manley, a married salesman who would be the last known person to see her alive. She was wearing a black tailored suit and a white blouse, carrying a black purse and a black suede shoe.

For six days, no one knew where she was. Then, on the morning of January 15, Betty Bersinger discovered her naked body in a vacant lot on Norton Avenue, in the Leimert Park neighborhood. The body had been severed cleanly at the waist, drained entirely of blood, and positioned with surgical precision. Her face bore the gruesome "Glasgow smile"—slashes from the corners of her mouth to her ears. Her intestines were tucked neatly beneath her buttocks. Her hands were raised above her head, elbows bent at right angles. Not a drop of blood stained the grass.

The killer had washed the body, transported it, and posed it like a mannequin in a window display. The precision of the cuts suggested medical knowledge. The positioning suggested artistry. The complete absence of blood suggested someone who knew exactly what they were doing. The press descended like vultures, and the legend of the Black Dahlia was born—one that would haunt Los Angeles for nearly eight decades.

THE QUESTION THAT HAUNTS EVERYONE:
Who had the surgical skill to bisect a human body with such precision? Who had the psychological composure to pose it in a vacant lot? And why—despite 192 suspects, thousands of tips, and decades of investigation— has no one ever been charged with the murder of Elizabeth Short? The LAPD officially lists the case as unsolved. But the files—what remains of them—tell a different story entirely.

Key Evidence Categories

Crime Scene

The Vacant Lot on Norton Avenue

Betty Bersinger discovered Elizabeth's bisected body at 10:30 AM on January 15, 1947. The body was positioned with grotesque precision—hands raised, legs spread, face turned toward the sidewalk. Not a drop of blood at the scene.

Medical Evidence

The Autopsy Findings

Dr. Frederick Newbarr's autopsy revealed the body was severed with surgical precision, all blood drained, organs removed and repositioned. The "Glasgow smile" was carved while Elizabeth was still alive. Ligature marks on wrists and ankles.

Communication

The Killer's Letters

Nine days after the murder, the LA Examiner received a letter: "Here is Dahlia's belongings. Letter to follow." It contained Elizabeth's birth certificate, social security card, and photographs. The handwriting was never identified.

Suspects

Dr. George Hodel

LAPD surgeon, abortionist, and friend of surrealist Man Ray. His son Steve Hodel, a retired detective, has spent decades building the case that his father was the killer. DA investigators in 1950 secretly recorded Hodel saying: "Supposin' I did kill the Black Dahlia?"

Last Known Contact

Robert "Red" Manley

The 25-year-old married salesman who drove Elizabeth from San Diego to Los Angeles and dropped her at the Biltmore Hotel on January 9. He was the LAPD's first suspect, subjected to a polygraph test, and eventually cleared—but questions remain.

Media Frenzy

The Press Circus

The LA Examiner, Herald-Express, and Daily News competed for sensational headlines. Reporters bribed LAPD officers for crime scene photos. Elizabeth's mother was tricked into a press conference with promises her daughter was alive. Ethics died with the Dahlia.

Case Timeline

Every known movement and event from Elizabeth Short's final days to the present investigation.

July 29, 1924
Birth in Boston

Elizabeth Short is born in Hyde Park, Boston, Massachusetts. She grows up in Medford, one of five daughters of Cleo and Phoebe Short. Her father abandons the family during the Depression, faking his own suicide.

December 1946
Arrival in San Diego

Elizabeth is living in San Diego, working as a waitress. She meets Robert "Red" Manley, a 25-year-old married salesman, at the Aztec Theatre. She tells him stories of Hollywood contracts and a fiancé named Gordon Fickling who is supposedly a Major in the Army Air Corps.

January 8-9, 1947
The Final Drive

Manley drives Elizabeth from San Diego to Los Angeles. They spend the night at the Figueroa Street Motel. The next morning, January 9, Manley drops her at the Biltmore Hotel downtown at approximately 6:30 PM. He watches her walk toward the lobby, carrying her black purse and single black suede shoe. He never sees her again.

January 9-14, 1947
The Missing Days

Elizabeth's movements during these six days remain unknown. Witnesses later claim to have seen her at various locations—the Crown Grill, the Aster Motel, the Biltmore—but none can be confirmed. The LAPD would spend decades trying to trace her steps. Theories range from her being held captive to voluntarily staying with someone she trusted.

January 15, 1947
Discovery

At 10:30 AM, Betty Bersinger discovers the body while walking with her daughter. LAPD detectives arrive to find the bisected corpse, posed with grotesque precision. The body has been washed, blood drained, and surgically severed. No blood at the scene. The temperature is in the 40s—cold enough to preserve the body but not freeze it.

January 24, 1947
The Killer's Package

The LA Examiner receives a package addressed to "Editor" with no return address. It contains Elizabeth's birth certificate, social security card, a newspaper clipping about her case, and photographs. The package was carefully wiped clean of fingerprints. A note reads: "Here is Dahlia's belongings. Letter to follow." No letter ever arrived.

1950
The Hodel Surveillance

DA investigator Frank Jemison and LAPD detective Harry Hansen place Dr. George Hodel under surveillance. They install a microphone in his home and record him saying to a companion: "Supposin' I did kill the Black Dahlia. They couldn't prove it now." The recording is lost for decades, rediscovered in the DA's files in 2003 by Steve Hodel.

2003-Present
The Investigation Reopened

Steve Hodel, George Hodel's son and a retired LAPD detective, publishes "Black Dahlia Avenger." Using his father's private photo album, DA files, and forensic evidence, he builds the case that George Hodel murdered Elizabeth Short and possibly others. The case remains officially unsolved, but the Hodel theory has gained significant traction among true crime investigators.

The Evidence

Forensic findings, crime scene analysis, and the physical artifacts that define the Black Dahlia case.

Forensic

Surgical Precision

The body was bisected with a single continuous cut, suggesting advanced anatomical knowledge. The incision followed the line of the lumbar vertebrae, avoiding bone. Dr. Newbarr noted the work suggested "someone with medical training, possibly a surgeon."

Forensic

Complete Blood Drain

Every drop of blood had been removed from Elizabeth's body. The killer either suspended the body upside down or used a pump system. The blood was never found—suggesting the murder occurred elsewhere and the body was transported to Norton Avenue.

Crime Scene

The Posed Mannequin

Elizabeth's body was arranged with disturbing artistry: arms raised above head, elbows bent at right angles, legs spread. Her face was turned toward the sidewalk, as if on display. The positioning suggests the killer wanted the body to be found and seen.

Evidence

Missing Personal Effects

Elizabeth's black purse, one black suede shoe, and her clothing were never recovered. The killer kept these items as trophies. Some theorists believe the missing shoe was kept as a memento, a common practice among serial killers.
