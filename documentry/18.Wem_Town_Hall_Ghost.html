WEM TOWN HALL
Wem, Shropshire — 19 November 1995

The Girl in the Fire

On a bitter November night in 1995, the old Victorian town hall in Wem, Shropshire, burned to the ground. Flames consumed the ninety-year-old building as firefighters battled the inferno and crowds gathered in the cold darkness to watch history crumble into ash. Among them was Tony O'Rahilly, an amateur photographer who raised his camera and captured what would become one of the most controversial ghost photographs ever taken. When the film was developed months later, standing in the doorway of the blazing building—unharmed, unafraid, impossibly calm—was the figure of a young girl in old-fashioned clothing, staring directly into the lens. She should not have been there. She could not have survived. Yet there she stood, the girl in the fire, and the town of Wem would never be the same.

The photograph lay dormant through the winter, tucked away in Tony O'Rahilly's darkroom in a shed behind his house. He was a sewage farm worker by trade, a photographer by passion, and a man who would soon find himself at the center of an international mystery. When he finally developed the black-and-white film shot with his 200mm lens that November night, he saw something that made his blood run cold. In the doorway of the burning town hall, behind the railings, stood a young girl in flowing garments. Her face was pale, her expression serene, her clothing from another era entirely. O'Rahilly had not seen her when he took the picture. No one in the crowd had seen her. The fire service's own video footage of the blaze showed no such figure. Yet there she was, as clear as the flames around her, a child standing in the heart of an inferno.

The town of Wem knew immediately who she must be. Three hundred and eighteen years earlier, in March 1677, a fourteen-year-old girl named Jane Churm had accidentally started a devastating fire in Wem. She had placed her candle too close to the thatched roof of her cottage while collecting firewood. The flames spread with terrifying speed, consuming buildings across the town, including the old Market House. Jane's guilt was said to have haunted her until her death, and local legend had long held that her tormented spirit lingered in the town hall that had risen from those ashes. Two workmen doing refurbishment in the old hall had previously reported being terrified by a ghostly figure shrouded in swirling mist. Now, it seemed, Jane had returned in the most dramatic way possible—standing in the doorway of the burning building as it collapsed around her.

O'Rahilly sent the photograph to the Association for the Scientific Study of Anomalous Phenomena (ASSAP), who forwarded it to Dr. Vernon Harrison, former president of the Royal Photographic Society. Harrison examined both the print and the negative and declared that the image showed no signs of tampering. "The negative is a straightforward piece of black-and-white work," he said, though he remained skeptical, suggesting it might be a trick of light and smoke. The BBC had the image analyzed by experts at the National Museum of Photography, Film, and Television in Bradford, who detected horizontal scan lines across the girl's face—suggesting the image might have been pasted from a television screen. But O'Rahilly maintained his innocence until his sudden death in 2005, never confessing to any manipulation. The mystery deepened, the legend grew, and Wem became known as a "Ghost Town" to visitors from around the world.

Then, in May 2010, a retired engineer named Brian Lear made a discovery that would shake the paranormal community. Looking through a nostalgia feature in the Shropshire Star, he spotted a 1922 postcard of Wem High Street. In the doorway on the bottom left stood a little girl bearing a striking resemblance to the "Wem ghost." The folds in her bonnet, the narrow sash around her waist, even the position of her right hand and thumb were identical. The ghost, it seemed, was a copy—a girl from a 1922 postcard superimposed onto the fire photograph. Tony O'Rahilly had died five years earlier, taking any confession with him. The bakery stopped selling ghost-shaped cakes. The road sign lost its "Ghost Town" addendum. But the image refused to die. It continues to circulate in books, documentaries, and online forums, a reminder that sometimes the most compelling ghosts are the ones we create ourselves.

THE QUESTION THAT BURNS STILL:
How does a girl from a 1922 postcard appear in a photograph of a 1995 fire? The Wem Town Hall ghost photograph remains one of the most debated paranormal images in history—a modern mystery that challenges our understanding of photography, deception, and the human need to believe in something beyond the flames.

Key Evidence Categories

Critical Evidence

The Ghost Photograph

The original 1995 photograph showing a young girl in old-fashioned clothing standing in the doorway of the burning Wem Town Hall. She appears calm and unharmed amid the inferno, wearing a bonnet and flowing dress from an earlier era. The image was captured on black-and-white film using a 200mm lens from across the road.

Primary Source

Tony O'Rahilly — The Photographer

An amateur photographer and sewage farm worker who captured the controversial image on the night of the fire. O'Rahilly maintained until his death in 2005 that he had not manipulated the photograph. He developed the film himself in a darkroom in a shed behind his house and was initially as puzzled as anyone by the girl's appearance.

Location

Wem Town Hall — The Burning Building

A Victorian-style red brick building completed in 1905 in the market town of Wem, Shropshire. The town hall housed a cinema operated by the Cheshire Animated Picture Company and later served as a community arts center. Its interior was completely destroyed by fire on November 19, 1995, though the Edwardian façade was later preserved.

Historical Figure

Jane Churm — The 1677 Firestarter

A fourteen-year-old girl who allegedly started a devastating fire in Wem in March 1677 by placing her candle too close to the thatched roof of her cottage while collecting firewood. The fire destroyed many buildings including the Market House. Local legend held that her guilt-ridden ghost haunted the town hall built on the site.

Smoking Gun

The 1922 Postcard — The Source

A vintage postcard of Wem High Street from 1922, reproduced in the Shropshire Star's "Pictures from the Past" feature. Retired engineer Brian Lear spotted a little girl in the doorway whose bonnet folds, dress sash, and hand position were identical to the "ghost" in the fire photograph. This discovery in 2010 exposed the image as a likely hoax.

Expert Analysis

Dr. Vernon Harrison — The Examiner

Former president of the Royal Photographic Society and psychical researcher who examined the original negative. He concluded the negative showed no signs of tampering but suggested the figure might be burning debris creating an optical illusion—a classic case of pareidolia. His analysis was both supportive and skeptical.

Case Timeline

Every documented event from the 1677 fire to the 2010 exposure and the decades of debate that followed.

March 1677
The Great Fire of Wem

Fourteen-year-old Jane Churm accidentally starts a devastating fire in Wem, Shropshire, by placing her candle too close to the thatched roof of her cottage while collecting firewood. The flames spread rapidly, destroying many buildings including the Market House. The fire is said to have been visible for miles around. Jane's guilt becomes local legend.

1905
Wem Town Hall Built

A new Victorian-style red brick town hall is completed in Wem, built on the site of the old Market House destroyed in the 1677 fire. The building features an Edwardian façade and houses a cinema operated by the Cheshire Animated Picture Company. An AWH Sound System is installed in 1934. The building becomes the center of community life in the market town.

Pre-1995
The Haunting Legend

Local legend holds that Jane Churm's guilt-ridden ghost haunts the town hall. Two workmen doing refurbishment in the old hall report being terrified by a ghostly figure shrouded in swirling mist that passes directly in front of them. The town hall fire of 1677 and Jane's alleged spectral presence become part of Wem's folklore, passed down through generations.

19 November 1995
The Night of the Fire

The interior of Wem Town Hall is completely destroyed by fire. Firefighters battle the inferno as crowds gather in the cold November night. Amateur photographer Tony O'Rahilly, stopped by police from approaching the building, takes photographs from across the road using a 200mm lens and black-and-white film. The fire service records video footage of the blaze. No one reports seeing a girl in the doorway.

Winter 1995–1996
The Discovery

Months after the fire, Tony O'Rahilly develops the black-and-white film in his darkroom shed. Examining the prints, he discovers the figure of a young girl standing in the doorway of the burning building. She wears old-fashioned clothing—a bonnet and flowing dress—and appears calm amid the inferno. O'Rahilly is stunned. He does not recall seeing anyone in the doorway when he took the photograph.

1995–1996
The Investigation Begins

O'Rahilly sends the photograph to ASSAP (Association for the Scientific Study of Anomalous Phenomena). The image receives extensive media coverage. Parasearch, ASSAP's regional group, is first on the scene to investigate. Experts are called in to examine the photograph and negative. The image quickly becomes linked with the legend of Jane Churm and the 1677 fire. "Ghostbusters" and paranormal investigators visit Wem from around the world.

1995–1996
Expert Analysis

Dr. Vernon Harrison of the Royal Photographic Society examines the negative and declares it shows no signs of tampering: "The negative is a straightforward piece of black-and-white work." However, he suggests the figure may be burning debris creating a simulacrum—a classic case of pareidolia. The BBC sends the image to the National Museum of Photography, Film, and Television in Bradford, where Paul Thompson and Will Stapp detect horizontal scan lines across the girl's face, suggesting the image was pasted from a television screen.

1995–2005
The Golden Age of the Wem Ghost

The photograph becomes one of the most famous and widely published ghost images in the world. It appears in books, documentaries, and paranormal television programs. Wem embraces its ghostly fame—the bakery sells ghost-shaped cakes, the road sign carries a "Ghost Town" addendum, and hundreds of extra visitors come to the town each year. Tony O'Rahilly becomes a local celebrity, maintaining his innocence and expressing curiosity about how the girl appeared. He gives interviews and allows continued examination of his work.

2005
The Photographer's Death

Tony O'Rahilly dies suddenly, taking any potential confession with him. He had never admitted to doctoring the photograph. Those who met him, including investigator Andrew Homer, believed O'Rahilly genuinely thought he was "acting in the best interests of Wem by bringing the town hall fire and the story of Jane Churm to the attention of the world's media." His death leaves the mystery unresolved but the legend intact.

2007
Digital Re-examination

Digital photography specialist Steve Potter examines a high-quality JPEG taken directly from the original negative by the Shropshire Star. He notes a denser area behind the figure that could indicate a thicker area of the original negative, suggesting "a selective enlargement which then was re-touched either on the print or by using an interneg." However, he discounts the television scan line theory, identifying the lines as scratch marks from dust and dirt in the camera's back—a common problem in 35mm cameras.

17 May 2010
The Postcard Discovery

Retired engineer Brian Lear, 77, spots a little girl in a 1922 postcard of Wem High Street reproduced in the Shropshire Star's "Pictures from the Past" feature. The resemblance to the "Wem ghost" is uncanny. Side-by-side comparison reveals identical folds in the bonnet, identical narrow sash around the waist, and the same right hand and thumb position. The ghost, it appears, was copied from a 1922 postcard. The paranormal community is shaken. Wem's ghost tourism industry begins to decline.

2010–Present
The Aftermath

The "Ghost Town" sign is removed. Ghost-shaped cakes disappear from the bakery. But the image refuses to die. It continues to circulate on social media, Reddit, TikTok, and YouTube, reaching new generations who debate its authenticity. Some argue that the postcard discovery doesn't definitively prove a hoax—perhaps the 1922 girl was also a ghost. Others point to an advertising sticker on the back of one of O'Rahilly's other photographs, suggesting he was more than the "very amateur photographer" he claimed to be, and capable of the techniques needed to create an interneg. The mystery endures, transformed but not extinguished.

Present Day
The Mystery Transformed

Multiple theories continue to compete: genuine paranormal manifestation of Jane Churm's ghost, accidental pareidolia in burning debris, deliberate hoax using the 1922 postcard, or a complex darkroom manipulation by O'Rahilly. The original negative has never been subjected to modern forensic analysis. The photographer is dead. The building has been rebuilt. But the girl in the fire—whether ghost, illusion, or fabrication—remains one of the most compelling images in paranormal history, a reminder that sometimes the truth burns brighter than the flames.

The Evidence

Physical findings, photographic analysis, and the inexplicable details that define the Wem Town Hall mystery.

Critical Evidence

The Ghost Photograph Itself

The 1995 photograph shows the Wem Town Hall engulfed in flames. In the doorway, behind the railings, stands the figure of a young girl in old-fashioned clothing. She wears a bonnet and flowing dress, her face pale and expression serene. She appears unharmed by the inferno around her. The image was captured on black-and-white film with a 200mm lens from across the road. No one at the scene reported seeing her. The fire service video shows no such figure.

Smoking Gun

The 1922 Postcard Source Image

Discovered by Brian Lear in 2010, this 1922 postcard of Wem High Street shows a little girl in a doorway whose features match the "ghost" exactly. The folds in her bonnet, the narrow sash around her waist, and the position of her right hand and thumb are identical. The image appears to be the source material for the ghost photograph, suggesting a deliberate superimposition rather than a paranormal manifestation.

Forensic Analysis

The Negative Examination

Multiple experts have examined the original negative. Dr. Vernon Harrison found no signs of tampering. The National Museum in Bradford detected horizontal lines suggesting television scan lines. Digital specialist Steve Potter later identified these as scratch marks from dust and dirt in the camera's back. A denser area behind the figure suggests possible selective enlargement or retouching. The negative remains the subject of conflicting interpretations.

Contradictory Evidence

The Fire Service Video

The fire service recorded video footage of the Wem Town Hall blaze on the night of November 19, 1995. This footage shows no sign of the little girl in the doorway, nor any burning debris that could have created the illusion described by Dr. Harrison. The absence of the figure in moving footage taken from a similar angle challenges both the paranormal explanation and the pareidolia theory, suggesting the girl exists only in O'Rahilly's still photograph.
